The surface level

Sports socialisation and
blending into daily life

In the powerhouses: hockey is part of the week

Interviews and chat sessions with international students studying in traditional hockey powerhouses proved that there is a dynamic blend of sport and schoolwork, where sport is an integral part of student life. In American high schools — including Cushing Academy, Lovell Academy and Lawrence Academy — students mentioned that the ice hockey season falls during winter break, which is suitable and preferable for students to attend.

Many students in these schools also take part in different sports during vacation, so they do not feel pressured or segregated. An interviewee in Japan noted that there are specific time slots reserved for hockey team players to focus on training and games, where they do not feel pressured by academic obligations. There are also plenty of opportunities for students to gain admission to top universities on the basis of both academic and ice hockey achievement.

In China: the "small circle"

In comparison, the ice hockey and broader sports communities in China exhibit a marked lack of socialisation. One of the most frequently mentioned phrases in the interviews is "small circle". Ice hockey is not integrated into modern daily life. As students get older, participation rates drop significantly and enthusiasm rapidly wanes — particularly at the age of sixteen, where academic pressure overwhelms most students so that they must choose between the identity of athlete or student.

Interviewees noted that schools run extra classes during summer and winter vacations, which occupy both students' resting time and their sporting time. As a result, there is still considerable room for change in China to enable youth to increase participation and retention in ice hockey and other sports.

Ice hockey is not integrated into modern people's daily life course. — Recurring theme across interviews

Moreover, for ice and snow sports China is still in a developing stage where most infrastructure — rinks, hockey stores — is not mature enough for wide participation. Based on questionnaire and research data, many southern cities and less developed provinces do not have a single ice hockey rink, which explains why China's national team is composed almost entirely of players from Harbin and Beijing in the north.

For the general Chinese population, the intrinsic meaning of sport — and ice hockey specifically — remains that of a hobby or playful activity, most frequently reduced to a utilitarian tool for physical fitness. Consequently, sport has failed to embed itself organically into the multifaceted aspects of daily life, such as mainstream education and commercial industry.

The next step: incorporating ice hockey and broader sports into students' lives, and making the identity of student-athlete exist in the real world.

Traditional powerhouses

As reported by many American high school students, the North American sports ecosystem operates through a fundamentally decentralised, community-embedded institutional framework. The model relies on an expansive grassroots infrastructure that integrates sport directly into the social, educational and commercial fabric of everyday life. Sociologically, local rinks and minor hockey associations function as vital "third places" — community hubs fostering social capital, civic identity and intergenerational bonding. Participation is not a utilitarian diversion or a hyper-specialised track for a select few, but a normative rite of passage and a primary vehicle for adolescent socialisation.

Institutionally, the United States and Canada offer ways to continue playing while progressing through school. In the US, the scholastic sports model — the NCAA in particular — transforms athletic participation from a liability into a highly valued asset for university admissions and scholarships. Instead of forcing adolescents to abandon sport in favour of academic rigour, the system rewards athletic excellence within education. Similarly, according to Jiayi (Ethan) Liu at Kuper Academy in Canada, Canada's tiered Minor Hockey Associations allow millions of youths to engage continuously across varying skill levels — from recreational house leagues to elite AAA and Major Junior circuits — ensuring a massive, organic participant base remains active long enough for elite talent to emerge naturally.

This structural integration is supported by a distinct pedagogical paradigm. Western team sports philosophies emphasise facilitative coaching and player autonomy, recognising that highly dynamic, high-uncertainty environments like ice hockey demand rapid on-ice improvisation. Coaching methodologies therefore prioritise "game sense" and tactical fluidity over mechanical repetition. Locker room cultures foster psychological safety and leadership, empowering core players to engage in real-time decision-making. The athlete is not passively learning but is an active, self-determining agent whose creative capacity is cultivated — essential to the team's competitive viability.

China

The reliance on "closed-door training" creates an atmosphere that is notably repressive compared with Western models; the disciplinary regime established by coaches and management contributes to declining retention and reduced passion. This approach, and the resulting contraction of the talent selection pool, inherently blocks the development of new ideas and experimental practice.

Based on interviews with China ice hockey national team players, closed-door training has a schedule that overlaps with normal high school hours from the age of sixteen, so students must choose between being a student or an athlete. This completely alters normative developmental trajectories, removing adolescents from traditional schooling to focus entirely on a fixed athletic pursuit — one of the major causes of the participation drop at this age.

Another reason is that sports leagues have been assimilated by the logic of the Gaokao. Professional training in the BHA (Beijing Hockey League) and other provincial leagues accepts only a relatively small percentage of players deemed talented or outstanding at U14 and U16 tournaments. These players then sign an agreement committing them to attend all training — even when it collides with school examinations. From that point there is a clear division between the identity of professional athlete and student. Because China's total hockey population is small and high-level series are directed mainly at provincial teams, there are essentially no seasonal tournaments available for amateurs, which discourages many enthusiasts. Recently, however, with a rising hockey population, larger leagues aimed specifically at semi-professional athletes and amateurs have begun to emerge.

These highly regimented selection and training methods are incompatible with the high-uncertainty, dynamic nature of team sports, rendering athletes overly disciplined and inflexible. As a result, a long-term talent chain and a robust sports industry have not been developed or sustained. With further growth in the hockey population, it is promising that better league systems — as in Western powerhouses — can be established to support players of all levels.

The root level

Structural & cultural
foundations of divergence

The foundational divergence between North American and Chinese youth sports ecosystems stems from differing historical pathways, educational paradigms and institutional incentives.

In North America, athletic participation is historically integrated into the framework of holistic human development. Sports are viewed as an indispensable component of education, fostering character, resilience and collaborative leadership. Backed by a mature commercial ecosystem and strong institutional support, athletics thrive organically as expressions of community identity and school pride — valuing both the athlete's holistic growth and competitive outcomes.

Conversely, the Chinese sporting landscape operates under a distinct set of cultural legacies and historical incentives. Historically, East Asian societies placed an overwhelming structural emphasis on standardised academic examinations as the single certified pathway for social mobility. In contemporary China, while the post-2022 Olympic era has catalysed immense public enthusiasm, this traditional academic meritocracy creates a formidable "environmental friction" against sustained athletic commitment, particularly as students approach high school.

Systemically, China's sports infrastructure was historically developed through a top-down, state-led model designed to optimise resource allocation for international athletic performance. Influenced by centralised training methodologies, this model naturally prioritised predictable, result-oriented outcomes over the organic growth of grassroots communities. While highly effective for elite Olympic success, this hierarchical structure inadvertently fostered a disciplinary training culture that constrains athlete autonomy, on-ice improvisation and long-term youth retention.

As China transitions toward a broader, more inclusive sporting paradigm, the central challenge lies in evolving from an outcome-driven system into an ecosystem that honours the intrinsic, holistic value of youth sport — bridging the gap between elite performance and lifelong community engagement.
